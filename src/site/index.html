Observational 3D model of the local universe

A 3D model of the universe with nothing invented in it.

Stellata places around 390,000 real objects — stars, planets, moons, the dust between them, the Milky Way, the nearest galaxies — at the positions astronomers have measured, and draws each one at the colour and brightness your eye would see it from wherever you happen to be standing. You fly through it in a browser tab.

Nothing in it is procedurally generated, artistically enhanced, or false-coloured. Where the measurements stop, so does the model.

§ 01

Three things this is, and one thing it refuses to be.

01

An instrument, built for people who already know the sky.

The controls are the ones an observer expects. Limiting magnitude derived from an aperture rather than a taste slider. Exposure in stops, so a change of one halves or doubles the light. Star sizes set by plate scale. An attitude indicator reading the camera against the ecliptic, equatorial or galactic frame, or against the focused object's own orbital plane. Coordinate spheres in all three frames with edge-labelled gridlines. The IAU constellation boundaries as Delporte drew them in 1930. A chart mode in the manner of Sky Atlas 2000.0, with Bayer and Flamsteed designations, double-star wings and variable-star rings.

Every view — camera pose, clock, filters, exposure, mode — packs into the address bar, so a view you found is a link you can put in a paper, an email or a lecture and know it comes back identically.

02

Every object came out of a published catalogue, and says which.

Positions, distances, proper motions, colours, temperatures, radii, pulsation periods, orbital elements, dust densities: each is read from published data, and the source is named in the application and in the repository. Star radii are computed from catalogued absolute magnitude and spectral class through Stefan–Boltzmann rather than assigned by eye. Variable stars pulse on their catalogued periods. Close binaries orbit on their measured elements. Every star is propagated from the epoch its position was measured at to the date on the clock, along its measured motion through space.

Theoretical predictions and conjectured structures are excluded on purpose. There is no procedural filler and no painted backdrop, which is also why the model has an edge: it currently reaches 6.5 million light years, and stops where the measurements do.

03

What your eye would see, from any point in the model.

There is no false colour anywhere in Stellata — no infrared, no radio, no narrowband palette, none of the composites that make published astronomy imagery beautiful and misleading about what is actually visible. Colour comes from measured temperature and photometry through the human visual response.

Brightness is handled the same way. Apparent magnitude is recomputed for the camera's real position every frame, so moving changes what you can see, and the light is attenuated and reddened by raymarching the actual three-dimensional dust map between camera and star. One global tone-map turns that into pixels, calibrated so a star at the naked-eye threshold sits at the threshold of your display. There is a calibration screen in the application for setting your monitor's black point, because on an uncalibrated screen in a lit room none of it means anything.

§ 02

The citation record is the product.

Thirty-four catalogues and datasets are credited inside the application, grouped by what they place. Behind them, the modelling record cites over 100 published papers with their DOIs — every formula, calibration and deliberate simplification written down and attributed, including the ones that make the model less pretty.

§ 03

Things worth going to look at.

Each of these exercises something the renderer does that a screenshot cannot show. Search for the object by name once the model is open.

Betelgeuse and Rigel
Both in Orion, both supergiants. Rigel is intrinsically brighter but hotter, so Stefan–Boltzmann gives it the smaller radius — the L = R²T⁴ trade-off, visible as two discs of different size. Approach slowly; they fill more of the view than you expect.
Albireo (β¹ + β² Cygni)
The famous gold-and-blue colour pair. In three dimensions it separates: 111 pc and 122 pc, about 35 light years apart along the line of sight, far too distant to be bound. The colour contrast is real, the pairing is a chance alignment — which is the modern post-Gaia consensus, and hard to see from Earth.
δ Cephei, Mira, η Aquilae
Open the time scrubber and accelerate the clock. Each swings in brightness, disc radius and colour together, on its own catalogued period.
Orion, from thirty parsecs off
The constellation lines are drawn from Earth's viewpoint. Move a few tens of parsecs and the figure deforms asymmetrically, because its stars were never at the same distance. This is the moment it stops being a planetarium.
The heliopause, from outside
Cross the upwind apex at about 122 AU and look back. The asymmetry Voyager and IBEX measured — roughly 115 AU at the flanks, 200 AU down the heliotail — only reads from out there.
Sol's system, at today's date
The eight planets and Pluto sit at their real heliocentric positions for the current time, with their major moons, atmospheres, axial tilt and rotation. The clock in the corner tells you which instant you are looking at.
Eight kiloparsecs above the galactic centre
The Milky Way is a bounded raymarch through galactic-scale density, not a skybox. Look down on the disc and bulge, then fly past: it reorients with proper parallax, and the mid-plane dust lane stays where it should.
Sagittarius dSph, the LMC, M31
Out to two megaparsecs. Sagittarius shows the elongated axis of a dwarf currently being torn apart; the Large Magellanic Cloud carries its measured 32° disc inclination rather than a generic ellipsoid.
§ 04

What it needs, and what does not work yet.

  • WebGPU is required. Chrome and Edge 113+, Safari 26+ (macOS Tahoe, iOS and iPadOS 26), Firefox 141+ on Windows and Apple-silicon macOS, Chrome for Android 121+ on Android 12+. A browser without it gets a page saying so rather than a blank canvas.
  • Desktop, for now. It loads and renders on a phone, but the interface is built around a keyboard and a pointer, and the small-screen layout is still to come. Two-finger rotate works on touch and on Safari's trackpad gesture.
  • The catalogue is a real download. Around 390,000 records arrive before the first frame, so the initial load is measured in seconds rather than instantly.
  • It is one person's spare time. Bug reports and suggestions are welcome in the issue tracker; outside pull requests are not being taken. Sponsorship, if the thing is useful to you, is warmly welcomed.
