A famous double that isn't one

Albireo appears as a gold-and-blue coloured binary pair visible through a small telescope. Seen in 3D, this optical double splits into two stars separated by over 30 light years, far too distant to be bound in an orbit. This beautiful colour pairing is a chance alignment seen from Earth, and the true separation can only be seen in simulation.

Live orbits for thousands of binary star systems

2,888 binary pairs are in the catalogue with astrometrically measured and verified orbital periods, eccentricity, inclination, and separation. View any measured system both live and under time acceleration to see the 3D dynamics of multiple star systems visible to the naked eye like Castor, Algol, and Alula Australis.

Sol's system at the current instant

All eight planets and Pluto sit at their real-time positions, including all major moons, atmospheres, axial tilt and rotation. The clock indicates the current moment in the simulation and can be accelerated and scrubbed anywhere from 3000 BCE to 3000 CE.

See the edge of the Sun's influence, shaped by the interstellar medium

The heliopause, a teardrop-shaped boundary around the Sun, marks the equilibrium point between its stellar energy and interstellar space. NASA's Voyager 1 and 2 probes measured it as they left our Solar System, crossing its outer edge in 2012 and 2018 respectively.

View the night sky from any star or planet

Use Observe mode to see the night sky as it appears from any star, moon, or planet in the catalogue. Stars appear at their local brightness, colour, position, and size according to your viewing location. See what Orion looks like from Tau Ceti, Vega, Aldebaran or Polaris.

The same catalogue drawn as a printed chart

Chart mode renders the sky as it would be set in a paper atlas from any location you're observing from, whether Earth or another star. Variable and binary stars are marked on the chart alongside molecular clouds, constellations, and your chosen celestial sphere grid reference.

Visualise the dust between the stars

Invisible to the naked eye, sparse but massive dust clouds dim and redden the light from distant stars. Both named molecular clouds and analytical dust volumes are incorporated into the model volume, accurately showing what you would see and how individual stars would appear from any vantage you're viewing from.

The record

The citation record is the product.

%VITE_SOURCE_COUNT% catalogues and datasets are credited inside the application, grouped by what they place. Behind them, the modelling record cites %VITE_REFERENCE_COUNT% published works, each held once in a citation index with the claims it is cited for. %VITE_VERIFIED_REFERENCE_COUNT% of them have been read from a copy of the work itself, every claim quoted verbatim with its page; the rest stay marked unverified until a copy can be read.

Every formula, calibration and deliberate simplification is written down and attributed, including the ones that make the model less pretty. Where a value departs from the paper it cites, the code gives both values and says why.

Before you click

WebGPU is required

Chrome and Edge 113+, Safari 26+ (macOS Tahoe, iOS and iPadOS 26), Firefox 141+ on Windows and Apple-silicon macOS, Chrome for Android 121+ on Android 12+. A browser without it gets a page saying so rather than a blank canvas.

Desktop, for now

It loads and renders on a phone, but the interface is built around a keyboard and a pointer, and the small-screen layout is still to come. Two-finger rotate works on touch and on Safari's trackpad gesture.

Around 980,000 records stream in brightest first: the naked-eye sky draws within moments, and the faint stars fill in over the seconds after.
